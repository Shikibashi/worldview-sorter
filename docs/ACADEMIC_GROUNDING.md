# Academic grounding: commitments, reasons and philosophical contrasts

## Scope and status

Release 0.9 adds 30 proposed distinctions and 90 original candidate items to the earlier bank. It is an academic content-model update, not a claim that 30 new psychological factors have been discovered. The detailed [construct/source/item matrix](../research/academic/CONSTRUCT_SOURCE_MATRIX.md) links every addition to its source, scope, item IDs and prohibited inferences. The source ledger distinguishes scholarly text, abstracts and metadata-only references.

The central design question is not merely what institutions a respondent favors, but **what claims and reasons support those preferences**. Moral realism measures can be contaminated by first-order moral judgments, universality, contextualism or epistemic confidence; those are not interchangeable with truth-aptness or stance-independence. The update therefore separates them rather than inferring a philosophical system from policy resemblance. See Pölzler, [How to Measure Moral Realism (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-018-0401-8), especially guidelines G1–G5, and Pölzler & Wright, [Anti-Realist Pluralism (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-019-00447-8).

## Corrections to the earlier comparison with Objectivism

**Fallibility is not a sufficient disqualifier.** Rand’s account permits error in propositional judgment. It distinguishes contextual certainty from infallibility; definitions can also change with knowledge. The new questions distinguish willingness to revise, fallibilism about knowledge, noninferential foundations, contextual certainty, conceptual usefulness and sensory-conceptual justification. None is treated as a universal anti-Rand switch. See Badhwar & Long, [Epistemology and Metaphysics, A.1–A.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ayn-rand/supplement.html).

**Universals are not the same issue as an external world.** Rand treats concepts as mental integrations based on reality, rather than simply asserting independently existing universals. The legacy OM03 bundle is deprecated. OM11 addresses abstract objects; OM12 universals; OM13 ordinary external-world independence; OM14 the method of ontological inquiry. These are separate questions. See Cowling & Giberman, [Nominalism in Metaphysics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nominalism-metaphysics/), and Badhwar & Long, [A.2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ayn-rand/supplement.html).

**Easy ontology is not automatically eliminativism or nominalism.** The approach proposes resolving existence questions through conceptual application conditions and ordinary empirical work. Its relation to realism is debated. The item set measures attraction to that method, not a predetermined inventory of objects. Marschall’s [Easy Ontology, Quantification, and Realism](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-019-02463-8) critically examines Thomasson’s proposed compatibility with realism. Thomasson’s [Ontology Made Easy](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/ontology-made-easy-9780190878665) is included as publisher-verified book metadata, not falsely represented as fully read here.

**Conditional reasons need not be arbitrary preferences.** The account of value in Rand relates value to living beings, and the choice-to-live issue is not equivalent to an unconditional command to survive. Different interpretations emphasize survival and flourishing differently. ME09 therefore measures human-life grounding; ME10 separately addresses life-conditional reasons. The latter is an optional, disputed comparison criterion, not a hard exclusion rule. See Badhwar & Long, [Ayn Rand, sections 1.3 and 2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ayn-rand/).

## Egoism, ownness and chosen concern

Psychological egoism describes ultimate motives; ethical egoism prescribes an ultimate moral aim; rational egoism concerns practical reasons. Allowing agents some priority for their own projects is weaker than requiring everyone to adopt an egoistic moral code. NE15, NE16 and AH13 separate these claims from the older NE10 prerogatives measure. See Shaver, [Egoism, sections 1–3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/egoism/).

NE17 is an explicitly provisional operationalization of ownness and revisable allegiance to ideals. NE21 separates elective concern for others from compulsory universal self-sacrifice. Neither measures greed, social isolation or inability to form relationships. The reference profile is restricted to a non-moralized reading of ownness, not a purported consensus reconstruction of every aspect of Stirner. See Leopold, [Max Stirner, sections 2.2–2.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/max-stirner/).

The benchmark fixture is a **generic synthetic respondent**, not an encoding of an identifiable user’s memories. It is possible to preserve strong market commitments while changing the ethical grounds, or to endorse chosen affection while rejecting a universal moral command to maximize self-interest. The tests must preserve those differences.

## Promises, exit, reliance and enforcement

A promise can be understood as a normative power, a practice-based commitment, or a source of expectations and reliance. Disagreeing about one account does not decide all the others. The update separates NE18 promissory self-binding, NE19 continuing endorsement and exit, NE20 reliance-based responsibilities, and PL33 contractual remedies. A permission to stop a personal service does not automatically eliminate compensatory responsibilities; conversely, a compensatory rule does not establish every theory of moral obligation. These are philosophical questions, not advice about any jurisdiction’s contract law. See Habib, [Promises](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/promises/), including its discussion of normative powers, expectations, practice and contract.

## Inquiry, foundations and social reality

EP16 measures pragmatic clarification by possible consequences. EP17 measures revisable concepts as instruments of inquiry. Neither implies that truth is whatever is convenient, that there is no external world, or that all pragmatists share a theology or ontology. The tradition contains important differences, including realist approaches. See Legg & Hookway, [Pragmatism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/).

EP18 concerns noninferential warrant, not immunity from error. EP20 separates sensory-conceptual justification from EP05’s a priori reliance. An endorsement of reason is therefore not scored automatically as rationalism in the historical a priori sense. See [Epistemology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology/) and the Rand supplement cited above.

SO14 separates an institution’s dependence on practices from a denial that it is real. SO15 separates individual-level social explanation from a moral priority assigned to individuals. The older SO02 group-agency/collectivism bundle is excluded from the reference profiles’ defining criteria and retained in the audit as unresolved content contamination. See Epstein, [Social Ontology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/social-ontology/).

## Rights, political obligation and institutional proposals

PL27 measures preinstitutional rights grounding; PL28 institutional or instrumental justification. These can coexist, and neither alone determines the strength of rights or the legitimacy of a state. A consequential justification of stable constraints need not prescribe direct welfare maximization in every case. See Wenar, [Rights](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rights/), particularly its treatment of the justification of rights.

The existing PL01 and PL02 remain separate. PL31 measures the possibility of useful institutions without a general moral duty to obey. PL32 measures an institutional preference for state abolition. PL29 concerns exclusive territorial legal authority, while PL30 concerns nonstate adjudication and protection. These distinctions prevent the classifier from assuming that every philosophical anarchist rejects voting, courts, all government services, or every transitional institutional compromise. See Dagger & Lefkowitz, [Political Obligation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/political-obligation/), and Peter, [Political Legitimacy, sections 2.1–2.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/legitimacy/).

Simmons’s [Justification and Legitimacy (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1086/233944) is recorded as a primary academic reference with metadata-only access in this pass; substantive exposition here relies on the reviewed scholarly entries. Hasnas’s [The Obviousness of Anarchy (2024), chapter 8](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780197784631.003.0009), is represented at publisher-abstract level as a practical argument for nonpolitical provision, not as proof that every proposed nonstate arrangement works. Ostrom’s [Beyond Markets and States (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.100.3.641) is not treated as a synonym for a program abolishing states. These are different institutional claims.

## What changed in matching

The old matcher accepted hand-assigned numeric targets for categorical constructs such as deity belief and naturalism. The replacement does not accept these coordinates at all. It compares exact raw item IDs and revisions to sourced, scoped criteria. It records support, opposition, neutrality, missing views and mixed evidence separately. A two-item corroboration rule is an authored engineering rule, not a calibrated psychological cutoff.

Nine non-exclusive reference comparisons are included: selected Objectivist commitments; a restricted ownness orientation; philosophical-anarchist obligation skepticism; nonstate legal provision; pragmatic inquiry; easy metaontology; ethical egoism; preinstitutional rights; and institutional rights justification. These do not exhaust philosophical traditions. No nearest winner or identity is assigned. Self-reported identities are retained separately, never used to fill in doctrine.

The evidence planner can identify which **actual bank items** are missing for a requested comparison. This is not calibrated computerized adaptive testing. It is not yet a public result-page feature, and the browser continues to withhold unvalidated scores.

## Published instruments versus this project

The Oxford Utilitarianism Scale distinguishes impartial beneficence from instrumental harm ([Kahane et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29265854/)). The Free Will Inventory distinguishes free-will, determinism and dualism beliefs ([Nadelhoffer et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24561311/)). MFQ-2 development is documented in [Atari et al. (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37589704/). They are relevant comparators, but their validation does not transfer to original items carrying similar labels.

Source grounding, three candidate items, an internally consistent rubric and passing regression tests are not respondent-sample validation. Reliability, dimensionality, invariance, item information and held-out classification performance remain empirical questions. The source register includes [Clark & Watson (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30896212/) as measurement-methodology metadata, without claiming access to a full text that was not retrieved.

## Versioning and remaining limitations

The 0.8 bank and its instrument remain byte-for-byte frozen. Three corrected legacy items receive incremented revisions. The old construct registry is archived, OM03 remains decodable but deprecated, and new releases receive separate bank, registry, pilot, scoring and profile versions. No historical answer is filled in or relabeled to match the new questions.

The source matrix and contrast-audit JSON explicitly preserve unresolved risks, including the older collectivism wording, response-method effects and incomplete diagnostic coverage in a short random packet. The update addresses the specified philosophical confusions; it is not a claim to have exhaustively audited every question in the entire bank.
