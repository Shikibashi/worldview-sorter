## Philosophical anarchism — general content-independent obedience criterion (scoped)

**Entity:** tradition
**Status:** partial; internal only
**Scope:** One comparison criterion: whether there is a general moral duty to obey law merely because it is law. This does not reconstruct philosophical anarchism as a whole.

### Mapped claims

| Claim | WVS target | Route status | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| general-content-independent-obligation | `legacy-philosophical-anarchism-no-general-obedience` (scope) — The philosophical-anarchist position about a general content-independent duty to obey, not a policy of immediate institutional abolition. | DIRECT: quick, standard, full | primary_text |

### Neighbors and non-entailments

**Nearby views:**

- A posteriori philosophical anarchism
- Consent-based accounts of political obligation
- Content-independent authority
- Prudential or moral reasons to comply without a general duty

**This comparison does not establish:**

- Immediate abolition of the state
- Rejection of voting, courts, or all institutions
- Violence, resistance, or refusal to comply in every case
- The claim that no law can create any moral reason for action
- Moral skepticism

### Deliberately unmeasured areas

- The full range of arguments for and against political obligation
- Institutional alternatives, transition, and the practical feasibility of anarchist arrangements
- Whether democracy, consent, fairness, or consequences can ground particular obligations

### Source trail

**general-content-independent-obligation**
- [In Defense of Anarchism](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/robert-paul-wolff-in-defense-of-anarchism) — Chapter I, section 3, conclusion on autonomy and a duty to obey laws simply because they are laws. Wolff's argument denies that citizens have a general duty to obey state laws solely because they are laws; this is one argument within philosophical anarchism.
- [Dagger & Lefkowitz: Political Obligation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/political-obligation/) — Section 3.2, 'Against Philosophical Anarchism.'. The scholarly overview treats philosophical-anarchist arguments as challenges to a general obligation to obey law and distinguishes variants of those arguments.

### Limitations

- This is a single-criterion comparison, not a complete philosophical-anarchist profile.
- Do not infer abolition, institutional rejection, tactics, or general noncompliance from this result.

## Pragmatism — pragmatic-maxim criterion (scoped)

**Entity:** tradition
**Status:** partial; internal only
**Scope:** The pragmatic maxim as a method for clarifying concepts and apparent disputes through conceivable practical or experiential bearings; not a complete account of pragmatism.

### Mapped claims

| Claim | WVS target | Route status | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| pragmatic-maxim | `construct-EP16` (proposition) — Disputed ideas should be clarified through their conceivable effects on experience or conduct; if two formulations have no conceivable difference in those effects, their apparent conceptual difference is not substantive. | ROUTE_LIMITED: full | primary_text |

### Neighbors and non-entailments

**Nearby views:**

- Generic fallibilism
- Scientific empiricism
- Instrumentalism
- Logical verificationism
- Consequences as one useful clarification technique

**This comparison does not establish:**

- A belief is true merely because it is useful
- A complete pragmatist theory of truth
- Rejection of mind-independent reality
- Reduction of all meaningful claims to observation
- A political or religious position

### Deliberately unmeasured areas

- Pragmatist theories of truth and realism
- The roles of inquiry, community, habit, and experimental practice across pragmatist traditions
- Differences among Peirce, James, Dewey, and later pragmatists

### Source trail

**pragmatic-maxim**
- [How to Make Our Ideas Clear](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html) — Part II, Peirce's statement and explanation of the pragmatic maxim. Peirce's maxim clarifies a conception by considering the conceivable practical bearings of its object.
- [Legg & Hookway: Pragmatism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/) — Sections 2 and 2.1; compare sections 3.1 and 3.2 for variation about truth. The scholarly account presents Peirce's maxim as a method for clarifying hypotheses through experience while documenting disagreement among pragmatists about truth and reality.

### Limitations

- The profile does not claim to measure every pragmatist doctrine or settle internal disagreement.
- The direct criterion is available only on the Full route; its omission on Quick or Standard is not opposition.

## Charles S. Peirce — pragmatic clarification and fallibilist inquiry (scoped)

**Entity:** philosopher
**Status:** partial; internal only
**Scope:** Two selected commitments in Peirce's work: pragmatic clarification and the fallibility of knowledge claims. This is not a whole-philosopher reconstruction.

### Mapped claims

| Claim | WVS target | Route status | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| peircean-pragmatic-maxim | `construct-EP16` (proposition) — Disputed ideas should be clarified through their conceivable effects on experience or conduct; if two formulations have no conceivable difference in those effects, their apparent conceptual difference is not substantive. | ROUTE_LIMITED: full | primary_text |
| peircean-fallibilist-knowledge | `construct-EP15` (proposition) — A claim can count as knowledge even when its justification leaves open a possibility of error. | ROUTE_LIMITED: standard, full | primary_text |

### Neighbors and non-entailments

**Nearby views:**

- Jamesian pragmatism
- Generic empiricism
- Fallibilism without the pragmatic maxim
- Pragmatism with different views of truth or realism

**This comparison does not establish:**

- Peirce's complete theory of truth or reality
- Skepticism or epistemic pessimism
- The doctrines of tychism, synechism, or agapism
- Peirce's semiotics, categories, or complete theory of inquiry

### Deliberately unmeasured areas

- Semiotics and the theory of signs
- Categories, normative sciences, and the architectonic structure of his philosophy
- Tychism, synechism, agapism, and the detailed account of truth and reality
- Abduction and the full logic of scientific inquiry

### Source trail

**peircean-pragmatic-maxim**
- [How to Make Our Ideas Clear](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html) — Part II, 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear.'. Peirce formulates the pragmatic maxim as clarifying a conception through the conceivable practical bearings of its object.
- [Legg & Hookway: Pragmatism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/) — Section 2.1, 'Peirce: The Pragmatic Maxim.'. The scholarly reconstruction identifies the maxim as central to Peirce's original conception of pragmatism.
**peircean-fallibilist-knowledge**
- [Fallibilism, Continuity, and Evolution](https://www.textlog.de/4248.html) — Collected Papers 1.141–1.175, especially section 1.141 on the limits of certainty in reasoning. Peirce argues that reasoning about factual matters cannot achieve absolute certainty; this claim is about fallibility, not that all beliefs are false.
- [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Fallibilism](https://iep.utm.edu/fallibil/) — Introduction and sections 2–3 on fallible justification and the possibility of knowledge. The scholarly account distinguishes the possibility of error in justification from skepticism and explains how fallible justification can be compatible with knowledge.
- [Legg & Hookway: Pragmatism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/) — Section 2, discussion of the fallibilist, anti-Cartesian norms of inquiry. The scholarly account connects Peircean pragmatism with fallibilist inquiry while distinguishing Peirce's framework from other pragmatist views.

### Limitations

- Two selected commitments cannot stand in for Peirce's broad and internally connected body of work.
- The pragmatic-maxim criterion is Full-only; Quick and Standard results leave that central criterion unmeasured.

## Empiricism about concrete external facts — experiential-warrant criterion (scoped)

**Entity:** tradition
**Status:** partial; internal only
**Scope:** One epistemic criterion: experiential evidence is ultimately required for knowledge claims about concrete external reality. It does not represent empiricism as a whole.

### Mapped claims

| Claim | WVS target | Route status | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| experiential-warrant-for-external-facts | `construct-EP20` (proposition) — Knowledge claims about concrete external reality ultimately require experiential evidence; reasoning alone cannot establish new external-world facts. | ROUTE_LIMITED: full | scholarly_reconstruction |

### Neighbors and non-entailments

**Nearby views:**

- Empiricism about the origin of concepts
- Rationalist accounts of a priori external-world knowledge
- Scientific empiricism
- Skepticism about the external world

**This comparison does not establish:**

- All knowledge comes from sensory experience
- Reasoning is unimportant or dispensable
- A denial of a priori knowledge in mathematics or ethics
- Naive realism or a complete theory of perception

### Deliberately unmeasured areas

- The source of concepts and the existence of innate ideas
- Whether a priori knowledge is possible in mathematics, logic, ethics, or other domains
- The structure of perception and the relation between experience and external objects

### Source trail

**experiential-warrant-for-external-facts**
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Rationalism vs. Empiricism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rationalism-empiricism/) — Sections 1.1–1.2, on empiricism and knowledge of the external world. The scholarly account frames empiricism and rationalism partly in terms of whether experience is required for knowledge of external-world facts, while allowing reasoning to organize evidence.
- [An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book IV](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/locke1690book4.pdf) — Book IV, chapter XI, especially sections 1 and 3–6. Locke distinguishes intuitive knowledge of one's existence, demonstrative knowledge of God, and sensitive knowledge of other things; this illustrates why the WVS comparison is restricted to external-world factual warrant.

### Limitations

- This criterion must not be presented as a complete empiricist worldview or as a classification of Locke.
- The criterion is available only on the Full route; omission from shorter routes remains unmeasured.

## Act consequentialism — direct maximizing rightness criterion (scoped)

**Entity:** tradition
**Status:** partial; internal only
**Scope:** One criterion: an act's own consequences directly determine its moral rightness, with a better available outcome ruling out a worse act. The profile does not identify utilitarianism or a theory of value.

### Mapped claims

| Claim | WVS target | Route status | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| direct-act-maximizing-criterion | `reviewed-NE22-act-consequence-criterion` (proposition) — An act’s own consequences ultimately determine its moral rightness, and an available act with a better outcome for everyone affected rules out a worse act as permissible. | ROUTE_LIMITED: full | scholarly_reconstruction |

### Neighbors and non-entailments

**Nearby views:**

- Full rule consequentialism
- Satisficing consequentialism
- Deontological constraints
- Generic outcome sensitivity

**This comparison does not establish:**

- Utilitarianism
- Welfarism
- Hedonism
- Impartial maximizing across all persons in every formulation
- An empirically calibrated moral type

### Deliberately unmeasured areas

- What counts as good or bad outcome value
- Whether welfare is the only intrinsic good
- The full scope of impartiality and aggregation
- How the criterion applies to demandingness, rights, and side constraints

### Source trail

**direct-act-maximizing-criterion**
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/) — Sections 1–2 and 5, distinguishing direct act criteria, maximization, rule criteria, and decision procedures. The scholarly account defines act consequentialism by the consequences of the act and a maximizing rightness condition, while distinguishing it from rule consequentialism and from decision procedures.

### Limitations

- This is a scoped criterion comparison; it is not a whole tradition profile and it does not imply a respondent identity.
- The single mapped criterion cannot establish utilitarianism, welfarism, or hedonism.

## Ethical egoism — exclusive moral self-priority criterion (scoped)

**Entity:** tradition
**Status:** partial; internal only
**Scope:** One narrow normative criterion associated with ethical egoism: each agent's own long-term good is the ultimate moral end and another person's need alone supplies no independent moral reason. This does not represent the standard maximizing if-and-only-if account of moral rightness.

### Mapped claims

| Claim | WVS target | Route status | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| exclusive-moral-self-priority | `construct-NE15` (proposition) — Each person morally ought to make their own long-term good the ultimate end of their actions; another person’s need alone supplies no independent moral reason. | ROUTE_LIMITED: full | scholarly_reconstruction |

### Neighbors and non-entailments

**Nearby views:**

- Standard maximizing ethical egoism
- Rational egoism about practical reasons
- Psychological egoism about motivation
- Self-regarding prerogatives or duties of self-care
- Stirnerian ownness

**This comparison does not establish:**

- That an act is morally right if and only if it best advances the agent's own good
- That people in fact always act selfishly
- That rationality always requires maximizing self-interest
- Indifference to others or refusal to help
- Rand's complete ethical system or any political position

### Deliberately unmeasured areas

- The standard maximizing if-and-only-if criterion of moral rightness
- Which theory of welfare or self-interest is correct
- Rule-, character-, satisficing-, or other nonstandard variants of ethical egoism

### Source trail

**exclusive-moral-self-priority**
- [Robert Shaver: Egoism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/egoism/) — Sections 1–3. The scholarly account distinguishes ethical egoism's moral focus on the agent's own good from psychological and rational egoism; its standard formulation is stronger than this scoped WVS criterion, so maximization remains explicitly unmeasured here.

### Limitations

- This is a one-criterion comparison and must not be presented as a complete ethical-egoism profile.
- Only Full can assess the active proposition; Quick and Standard omission remains not measured.

### Source trail

- [Badhwar & Long: Ayn Rand](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ayn-rand/) — Section 2.1. The scholarly overview describes Rand's ethical theory as grounding value in the requirements of human life while situating that claim inside a larger system.

### Limitations

- This profile deliberately promotes one exact Rand-specific commitment, not Objectivism as a system.
- Only Full can assess ME09; shorter-route omission must remain not measured.

**Entity:** philosopher
**Status:** partial; internal only

| mill-general-happiness-ultimate-standard | `reviewed-NE26-general-happiness-ultimate-standard` (proposition) — General happiness is the ultimate moral standard: moral rules, duties, and judgments are ultimately justified or resolved by their relation to the general happiness, without requiring that standard to be applied directly to each individual act. | ROUTE_LIMITED: full | primary_text |

**Nearby views:**

- rule consequentialism

**This comparison does not establish:**

### Deliberately unmeasured areas

### Source trail

**mill-general-happiness-ultimate-standard**
- [John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism, Chapter II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11224/11224-h/11224-h.htm) — Chapter II. Mill holds that the Greatest Happiness Principle is the foundation of morality, treating general happiness as the ultimate moral standard while recognizing secondary principles.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Mill's Moral and Political Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mill-moral-political/) — Sections 2.6–2.10. Scholars distinguish Mill's general happiness standard from decision procedures and record competing direct, rule, and sanction reconstructions.

### Limitations

- This comparison tests only the first-principle status of the general happiness standard.
- It does not classify respondents as utilitarians or determine act vs rule consequentialism.
- The proposition is evaluated only on the Full route; Quick and Standard routes leave it not measured.
